Why this is a white-hat view

This view asks how primary-school enrollment differs between girls and boys within economies, rather than treating a single average as a complete account of equality. It uses the World Bank gender-indicator dataset linked in the assignment, specifically the adjusted net enrollment rates for female and male primary-school-age children in 2017. The analysis retains only economies with a region in the accompanying country metadata and nonmissing observations for both sexes. Regional, income-group, and world aggregates are excluded to avoid counting their populations again alongside individual economies. The resulting sample contains 80 economies; it is not a census of the world.

Each economy receives two position-encoded dots on the same fixed 0–100% scale. Connecting the dots makes the within-economy difference visible without implying uncertainty. The adjacent gap label is calculated as female minus male enrollment in percentage points, not a percent change. Sorting by this difference reveals both female and male disadvantages. Region selection and text search are explicit, reversible filters; the count, summary statistics, table, and chart update together. The mean gap is an unweighted mean of economy-level differences, not a population-weighted global enrollment gap. The largest absolute gap is also shown so that averaging does not hide disparity.

Color distinguishes the two reported categories consistently, while numeric labels and a keyboard-accessible table provide alternatives to color. These indicators describe enrollment, not attendance, learning outcomes, safety, or all dimensions of gender equality. Binary sex categories reflect the source, not the full range of gender identities. Missing observations and differences in national reporting limit comparisons. The dataset is a historical repository snapshot, not a claim about current conditions. No causal conclusion follows from these differences.

How the black-hat frame works

The second view makes a sweeping claim about gender equality while displaying a deliberately favorable slice of the same 2017 observations. The selection first keeps economies where female enrollment is at least male enrollment, then sorts by female enrollment and retains the top eight. That procedure is not a representative sample, a predeclared regional comparison, or a measure of worldwide progress. Yet the language “leading education markets” makes this outcome-based selection sound like a meaningful external classification. The small source note acknowledges selection without explaining its decisive rule.

The headline and subtitle then generalize from those eight economies to primary education as a whole. The prominent “100%” statistic is arithmetically correct only for the featured subset and the chosen test of female enrollment being at or above male enrollment. It does not mean that every child is enrolled, that every economy has parity, or that gender equality has been achieved. Moreover, a female advantage can itself be a gap rather than equality. The large, celebratory metric and polished report styling direct attention toward the favorable interpretation before readers inspect those qualifications.

The paired dots intentionally retain accurate values and a common 0–100% axis. This makes the manipulation less conspicuous than a broken axis or fabricated observation: the distortion occurs through data selection and titles, not false arithmetic. The audit button replaces the subset with all 80 eligible economies and discloses the exact selection rule, omitted count, and full-sample range. The restored view shows why this reassuring conclusion is unwarranted. Even the full sample cannot establish global gender equality, because it excludes economies with missing values and measures enrollment alone. The exercise illustrates how a truthful chart can still carry an untruthful argument when a convenient denominator and an expansive headline do the persuasive work.

Source snapshot: World Bank gender indicators, curated repository. Retrieved November 12, 2026. We use 2017 even though this repository contains later years.

Original gender.csv · Country metadata · Extracted observations

Both rates must be present; a nonempty metadata region identifies economies rather than aggregates. No imputation, population weighting, or rounding occurs before analysis. Display values are rounded to two decimals. The extraction script is included in the code package.

Indicator definitions: female (SE.PRM.TENR.FE) and male (SE.PRM.TENR.MA). World Bank indicators draw on UNESCO Institute for Statistics education data. This repository is a secondary copy and may differ from later revisions.
